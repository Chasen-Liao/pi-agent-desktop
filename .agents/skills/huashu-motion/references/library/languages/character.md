# character — someone the viewer cares about

A character (a mascot, a little agent, a voxel creature, a rubber-hose figure) acts out the story:
Opus delegating small tasks to Haiku, an agent waking up at its desk, a cube demonstrating the 12
principles of animation.

**Says:** *emotion, humour, personality; this product has a heart.*

**Use when:** stories, consumer and kids' products, brand personality, explaining agents (agents as
characters is intuitive), social content. **Avoid when:** the audience needs facts fast.

## Signature

1. Clear silhouette and a small set of expressions (eyes do most of the acting).
2. The 12 principles, visibly: anticipation, squash & stretch, overlap, follow-through, arcs, ease.
3. A simple world (a desk, a stage, a diorama) with props that matter to the story.
4. Story beats with a turn and a payoff (a gag, a reveal, a callback).

Variant, the vignette: a self-contained little scene with no product in it (a pelican on a bike, a
quote from a film, four seasons through a train window). It spreads on mood or a joke, and is a good
C direction for a brand with a sense of humour; it does not explain anything. In launch films,
animated characters were absent in Oct 2026: stories there were told by real people.

## Camera · rhythm · easing

Staging like animation: wide to establish, medium for action, close for reaction. Holds after
punchlines (storytime timing). Springs with real overshoot for bodies.

## Sound

Foley for every action (steps, pops, boings), a playful score, maybe a voice or gibberish.

## Build

`storytime` template for first-person stories; SVG/Canvas rigs (bones as nested groups) or Three.js
for voxel/3D characters; draw characters well — don't draw people with code primitives when the style
needs real character art (generate the art, then animate it).

## Breaks when

Characters with no goal; stiff linear motion; uncanny faces; a mascot added to a film that doesn't need one.
